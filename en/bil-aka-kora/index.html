The artist

Who is he?

A poem written in French, translated here.

Listen.
Before the voice, there is the earth.
Before the guitar, there is the drum.
And before the name, there is Pô.

It was the twenty-fifth of April, nineteen seventy-one,
in the south of the country, where the Nahouri keeps watch over the border.
A mother gave the world
one more son among seventeen.
They named him Bilgho Akaramata Kora.
The world, in more of a hurry, would say Bil Aka Kora.
He grew up in the Kassena courtyards,
where ceremonial songs keep time,
where you learn rhythm
before you learn words.

One day he went up to Ouagadougou
to study numbers,
mathematics, physics,
the laws that hold the world upright.
But rhythm cannot be solved as an equation.
In nineteen ninety-seven,
at the National Grand Prix,
the country heard him,
and the first prize for modern song
told him: your road is here.

His first album came in ninety-eight.
He called it Douatou, the rainmaker.
The one who makes the rain fall
makes the millet rise, and the millet feeds the village.
He gathered his brothers of the stage,
the Djongo System,
for Djongo is never sung alone.

They say Djongo was the dance of strong men,
the one where young men showed their vigour
under the gaze of young women.
Bil took it in his hands
and set it beside jazz, reggae and blues.
They looked at each other for a long time,
then they knew each other,
like cousins the ocean had kept apart.

Then came Ambolou, “what is dear to me”.
Then Dibayagui, carried high by the public.
Twice the Kundé d'Or crowned him,
in two thousand and two, then in two thousand and five.
Then Yaaba, long in the making alongside Ray Lema.
Then an album whose title simply says:
leaving, and coming back.

Ghana and Mali, Togo and Côte d'Ivoire,
France, Belgium, Germany, Austria, Canada.
The stages change language,
but the drum speaks the same one everywhere.
Whoever leaves with the rhythm of his village
never entirely goes away.
He always comes back.

In two thousand and eight, he lit up the Nuits Djongo,
residencies where the instrumental music of Burkina
learns to stand on its own feet,
where the youngest receive what the elders have passed on.
For a tree that gives no seeds
is only a memory of forest.
And when the music falls silent,
he also lends his voice and his body
to film and to theatre.

Bil, son of Pô.
Bil, wide voice.
Bil, rainmaker.
Bil, bearer of Djongo.
Listen.
The drum has not finished speaking.

The style

Djongo

In southern Burkina Faso, in Kassena country, Djongo is first of all a dance. A dance of strength and stamina, in which young men once proved their vigour before the whole village, carried by drums and song.

Bil Aka Kora grew up in those courtyards. From the dance he kept the pulse, the rhythm you learn before you learn words. He set it in conversation with music born on the other side of the Atlantic: jazz, reggae, blues. Cousins the ocean had kept apart, recognising one another.

It is neither one more fusion nor folklore behind glass. A wide, open voice singing in Kassena, Dioula, sometimes Italian; a nylon-string guitar; and underneath it all, the stubborn stride of Kassena rhythm. On stage, the Djongo System does what the dance always did: it gets the room on its feet.
